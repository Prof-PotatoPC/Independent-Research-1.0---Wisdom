Independent dentistry research plan · Literature checked November 25, 2026 · Planning stage

**Finding.** I found no human intervention study demonstrating that a medicine selectively dissolves a fully formed adult third molar in place. There are public records of researchers chemically interrupting tooth development in animals, observing spontaneous resorption in people, and enzymatically loosening teeth in excised pig jaws. Each offers a plausible piece of the story. None identifies what the clinicians originally heard or supports the claim that someone suppressed a treatment. [1966 chemical experiment](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345660450020401) · [Adult human case](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3146626/) · [Pig-jaw extraction study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225053/) · [Targeted human trial registry search](https://clinicaltrials.gov/api/v2/studies?query.cond=%28%22third%20molar%22%20OR%20%22wisdom%20tooth%22%29&query.intr=%28collagenase%20OR%20%22tooth%20dissolution%22%20OR%20%22enzymatic%20extraction%22%29&countTotal=true&pageSize=10&fields=NCTId%2CBriefTitle%2CInterventionName&format=json)

## Follow the tissue and the endpoint

| What happened? | What researchers actually observed | What it tells us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A chemical interrupted development | Selinger, Archer and Thonard (1966) studied sodium tetradecyl sulfate in developing premolars of 15 dogs. The published synopsis reports inhibited eruption, residual abnormal root development in three teeth, and inflammatory and degenerative changes. | This is a historical, identifiable chemical lead. The teeth were immature dog premolars. The injury and residual development matter when interpreting the word “disappear.” [Publisher synopsis](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345660450020401) |
| A wisdom tooth resorbed spontaneously | In a 12-year-old, imaging documented complete pre-eruptive crown resorption, with absence of the third molar on follow-up. In a 57-year-old, microscopy after extraction documented resorption of the crown of an unerupted wisdom tooth. | The first case documents disappearance during development. The second documents a partially resorbed adult tooth that still required removal. Neither patient received a drug to cause it. [Pediatric case abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25514071/) · [Adult case abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3146626/) |
| An enzyme made a root easier to extract | Two linked studies measured how much pulling force released pig premolar roots after treatment of the surrounding ligament. | These are the closest published matches for a medicine assisting extraction. The recorded outcome was mechanical force in newtons. [2022 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225053/) · [2023 follow-up](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10353231/) |
| Energy disrupted a tooth bud | One pig study examined microwave ablation of third-molar buds. A follow-up tracked healing over 28 days using **second**-molar buds in juvenile pigs. | Developmental stage and tooth identity matter even across papers from one research program. This is a device approach. [Third-molar study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35709973/) · [Follow-up abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36592934/) |
| A gel helped remove decay | A human randomized trial compared papain-based gels and other methods for excavating carious tissue. | A dental “dissolving gel” headline may describe removal of decayed dentin while preserving the tooth. [Clinical study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11375084/) |

The biology behind natural shedding is real. Cells called odontoclasts resorb the dentin and cementum of baby-tooth roots as the teeth loosen and shed. Permanent teeth can also undergo pathological resorption. In human imaging studies of impacted wisdom teeth, a clinically important finding is damage to the root of the *neighboring second molar*. A proposed adult treatment would need evidence about which tissue is affected, how much remains, and what happens to the neighboring tooth, jawbone, gum, nerve and healing socket. A simulation supplied with an assumption that only one tooth is affected cannot establish that selectivity. [Physiological resorption review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9005890/) · [Human imaging study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10635620/)

The clinical reason to intervene also matters. Some impacted wisdom teeth remain free of disease. UK NICE guidance restricts surgical removal of impacted third molars in the NHS to cases with evidence of pathology. A Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to determine whether asymptomatic, disease-free impacted teeth should routinely be removed or retained. For an extraction-assistance study, the relevant future population would be people who already have an indication for removal. [NICE recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ta1/chapter/1-Recommendations) · [Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003879_surgical-removal-versus-retention-management-asymptomatic-disease-free-impacted-wisdom-teeth)

## The protein lead and its prior art

**Collagenase G (ColG)** acts on collagen. The periodontal ligament is a band of collagen-rich tissue that attaches the root to its bony socket. The studies applied the enzyme locally around separated roots in excised pig jaws. An oral medicine that could recognize one adult wisdom tooth would require an entirely different demonstration of delivery and specificity. Even complete loosening of the ligament would leave the physical obstruction of overlying bone or an unfavorably angled impacted tooth. [2022 study, introduction and methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225053/) · [Third-molar anatomy and impaction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8925877/)

| Proposed improvement | Existing evidence | Open question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reduce the effort of extraction | The 2022 split-mouth study reported about **20% lower average extraction force** for ColG-treated roots compared with the corresponding roots treated with vehicle. Forces and responses varied across roots and jaws. | How consistent was the reduction, and did a few jaws dominate the average? Patient outcomes such as fractures, pain and healing were outside this experiment. [Figures 3 and 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225053/) |
| Improve protein stability | In 2023 the same group reported an engineered variant with a melting temperature nearly **4 °C higher** and about **11% lower average extraction force compared with the earlier enzyme**. The paper gives root-specific reductions of 12%, 13%, 8% and 6%. | The percentages in the two studies use different comparators. Adding them would misstate the evidence. Melting temperature alone cannot establish shelf life in a formulation. [Results and Figure 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10353231/) |
| Lower cost | The reviewed studies supply laboratory endpoints. They do not establish a manufacturing cost or cost per successful clinical extraction. | A later cost analysis would need sourced production yield, purification and quality-testing costs, shelf life, clinician time, and a conventional-extraction comparator. Without those inputs the cost advantage is unknown. [2022 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225053/) · [2023 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10353231/) |
| Use a different mechanism | Natural resorption can remove hard tissue. Mechanical loading and root attachment area also influence extraction forces in an independent swine biomechanics study. | The latter gives a concrete competing explanation for variation in force. Different tooth types and experimental setups limit direct comparison. [Resorption review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9005890/) · [Independent mechanical study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11745890/) |

A public US patent describes using an agent such as collagenase on tissue around a tooth before extraction. Ligament-assisted extraction and computational stabilization of this enzyme are already disclosed. A patent records what its inventors claimed. Experimental papers are needed to assess what was demonstrated. This scoped review cannot establish worldwide novelty, patentability or freedom to operate. The proposed first contribution below is a reproducible analysis of published evidence. [US10016492B2, abstract and claims](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10016492B2/en) · [Engineered variant](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10353231/)

## Why some people never develop wisdom teeth

**Agenesis** means the tooth never formed. **Impaction** means it formed but could not erupt normally. Prior extraction and late development can also explain an empty space or an apparently absent tooth on a single image. A credible cohort comparison needs extraction history, suitable radiographs and comparable ages at assessment. [Human genetics study, ascertainment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24172245/) · [Updated prevalence review, eligibility and limitations](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003996926001688)

- A 2026 review pooled 125 data points covering **87,282 people**. An estimated **23.07%** lacked at least one third molar (95% confidence interval 21.2–25.0%). The authors found no significant difference between the earlier dataset and studies added from 2015–2025. Study estimates varied considerably. The newer review incorporates earlier evidence, so comparing its headline estimate with the previous 22.63% cannot establish an increase across generations. To test such a trend, compare birth cohorts from the same catchment with matched ascertainment. [2026 review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003996926001688) · [Earlier review abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25883107/)
- There is a genetic contribution. A family study linked an **MSX1** variant to missing second premolars *and* third molars. A twin study also found substantial heritability within its sample. Neither provides a sequence that determines wisdom teeth in everyone. [Family study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8696335/) · [Twin study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5974231/)
- A Japanese and Korean genome-wide study included 149 cases and 338 controls. Its strongest association lay in **THSD7B**. The authors called it a candidate for replication. A variant associated with development does not explain how to remove a mature tooth. [Study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24172245/)

Regeneration is a separate, active question. An anti-USAG-1 antibody rescued tooth formation in mouse models of congenital agenesis. The Japanese registry lists a completed phase I study of TRG035 in healthy adults and identifies **safety** as the primary endpoint. That record cannot establish that the drug reliably replaces an extracted human tooth. [Animal study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7880588/) · [Japanese phase I registry](https://rctportal.mhlw.go.jp/en/detail?trial_id=jRCT2051240154)

## A first project you can do with a spreadsheet

**Working question.** In the published pig premolar experiments, how large and consistent is the paired reduction in peak extraction force across root positions and jaws? Can a reader reproduce the headline estimates from the information that is public?

A strong result could be a reproducible estimate, an explanation for why estimates differ by root, or a precise account of which comparisons the public data support. No wet lab, clinical records or human participants are needed. The calendar below is a suggested four-week plan.

| When | Work | Deliverable and check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Week 1 | Start with Figure 4A and sections 2.5 and 3.2 of the 2022 paper, then Figure 3B of the 2023 paper. Make a row for every recoverable treated/comparator root pair. Record paper, panel, jaw ID if visible, root label, both forces in newtons, comparator, exclusions, data source and reading uncertainty. | A spreadsheet and a short codebook. The 2022 methods list **11 + 11 + 12 + 12** evaluable pairs, while the results discuss **44** in a particular summary. Audit the panel and denominator for each estimate. Leave unresolved counts and ID fields explicit. [2022 figures and methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225053/) · [2023 figures and methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10353231/) |
| Week 2 | Prefer tabulated values. If only a figure is available, calibrate its axes, digitize it twice and keep the readings. Preserve pairs only where the figure identifies them. Label digitized values approximate. The 2022 raw data are available on request and the 2023 article points to its publication and supplement. | A data provenance column for every value. If points overlap or lines cannot be traced, retain the published summary and explain the ambiguity. Never fill in a guessed root or jaw ID. [2022 data statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225053/) · [2023 data statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10353231/) |
| Week 3 | For each pair calculate absolute reduction as comparator force minus treated force, and percent reduction as 100 times that difference divided by comparator force. Within each paper compare the mean of individual percentages with the percentage calculated from the two group means. Summarize by root type. If jaw IDs are recoverable, average within jaws, then omit one jaw at a time to see how much it shifts the result. | One table per comparator and a paired plot with newtons on the axis. Roots from one animal share a jaw. Keep the animal as the independent unit for uncertainty estimates when identifiable. If jaw IDs are unavailable, report descriptive summaries and limit the inference. |
| Week 4 | Check the spreadsheet calculations by hand for three pairs. Record ambiguities, exclusions, possible digitization error and whether the direction or magnitude changes across reasonable summaries. Search for prior reanalyses before claiming originality. | A brief methods-and-results paper with the spreadsheet, figures and a reproducibility note. Label the result as a secondary analysis of animal data. A null finding or an irreconcilable denominator is reportable if the evidence trail is clear. |

**First sitting.** Open the 2022 paper at Figure 4A. Pick one jaw and root position with two clearly readable points. Copy the axis units and both values into your sheet. Identify the control from the legend, calculate that pair's difference, then check whether the rest of the panel can be read the same way. That single exercise tests the data availability assumption before you invest in coding. [Figure 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225053/#jfb-13-00076-f004)

**Decision before a tissue simulation.** If the measurements are recoverable, a later mechanical model could ask whether it predicts extraction force in held-out specimens from a defined experimental setup. Start with a baseline predicting the training-set mean for each root position. Reserve entire jaws for evaluation and report prediction error in newtons. An independent study of 49 swine incisor specimens already links attachment area and loading rate to force and tests a finite-element model against measurements. Its anatomy and loading experiments make it a reference for model design. Human third-molar validation would require comparable, independently measured human outcomes. If the paired data cannot be recovered, finish the evidence audit and document the missing measurements. [Biomechanics study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11745890/)
